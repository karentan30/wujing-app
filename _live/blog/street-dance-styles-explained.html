8 Street Dance Styles Explained: From Breaking to Popping

By Wujing Team · August 2025 · 8 min read

Street dance is not one thing. It's a family of distinct styles that evolved in specific cities, specific decades, and specific communities — each with its own vocabulary, music, and culture. Lumping them together as "hip-hop dance" is like calling every Italian dish pasta. This guide breaks down 8 foundational street dance styles, where they came from, what their essential moves look like, and how you can start learning each one.

1. Breaking (B-Boying / B-Girling)

Origin: The South Bronx, New York, early 1970s. DJ Kool Herc isolated the percussion break of funk and soul records, creating the musical space that B-boys and B-girls danced to.

Structure: Breaking is built from four elements. Toprock is standing footwork — the upright section where you establish rhythm before going to the floor. Downrock (also called floorwork or footwork) is floor-based patterns: six-step, three-step, CC's. Power moves are the acrobatic spectacles — windmills, headspins, flares, and airflares. Freezes are held positions that punctuate the end of a combination, like a baby freeze (balanced on one elbow), an airchair, or a headstand freeze.

How to start: Begin with toprock — specifically the Indian step and the cross-step — before touching the floor. Toprock is underrated; judges and audiences notice immediately when someone's toprock has no groove. Once you have rhythm in your toprock, learn the six-step for downrock. This is the alphabetical foundation of every floor sequence.

Music: Original breakbeats — James Brown, Incredible Bongo Band. Modern cyphers also use current hip-hop and trap with heavy breakbeats.

2. Popping

Origin: Fresno, California, early-to-mid 1970s. Sam Solomon (Boogaloo Sam) and his Electric Boogaloos crew developed the style, which spread through Soul Train television appearances.

Core technique: Popping is built on a muscle contraction — a quick flex and release — called a "pop" or "hit." The hit should be sharp enough to visibly snap the body on the beat. From this single technique, an entire vocabulary grows: Hitting is the basic hit on every beat. Dime stops are abrupt halts in motion — you move, then freeze instantaneously as if you hit a wall. Waving moves a contraction sequentially through the body like a ripple — arm wave, body wave. Animation mimics stop-motion movement, as if you're being moved frame by frame.

How to start: Isolate the arm pop first. Stand relaxed, then contract your bicep and tricep simultaneously, snap the elbow straight, and release. Once you can hit cleanly with one arm, practice both arms together, then alternate. This single move, done cleanly on the beat, is the entire foundation.

Music: Funk, electro-funk, and post-disco — Zapp and Roger, Daft Punk, and contemporary R&B with syncopated drums.

3. Locking

Origin: Los Angeles, 1969. Don Campbell accidentally created locking when he forgot the steps to the Funky Chicken and started freezing mid-move on the beat. His crew, The Lockers, formalized it.

Character: Locking is theatrical and comedic — it rewards personality as much as technique. The hallmark move is the lock: an abrupt stop in a wide, open position with bent elbows. Other vocabulary includes points (pointing emphatically at someone or something), Scooby Doos (a knee-knocking, inward-turning step), wrist rolls, and the Alpha (a signature arm isolation). Locking is interactive — dancers often play off the audience and each other mid-performance.

How to start: Learn the lock position itself: arms out, elbows bent at roughly 90 degrees, one hand pointing. Hold it for a beat with confidence. Then add wrist rolls between locks. Finally, start moving across the floor with basic locking footwork. The tempo is usually fast, so drilling slowly first is essential.

Music: Funk — James Brown, Parliament-Funkadelic, Kool and the Gang. The music must have a strong downbeat to lock onto.

4. Waacking

Origin: The underground gay clubs of Los Angeles in the early-to-mid 1970s, particularly among Black and Latino LGBTQ+ communities. It gained wider visibility through Soul Train in the late 70s and experienced a global revival after 2010.

Core moves: Waacking revolves around fast, whipping arm movements — the arm swings up and around at speed, punctuating musical accents. Posing is the other pillar: dramatic, fashion-inspired body poses held between arm swings, often referencing classic Hollywood glamour. The style has a strong connection to the music — arms punctuate hits and phrase endings, not just the downbeat.

How to start: Practice a single arm swing — bring the arm up from below, swinging past your face at speed, and stop it cleanly. The stopping point matters as much as the swing. Then practice the basic two-arm alternating pattern while listening for musical accents to hit.

Music: Disco, Hi-NRG, and contemporary house music. Donna Summer, Gloria Gaynor, and modern house producers.

5. Krump

Origin: Compton, Los Angeles, early 2000s. Created by Tight Eyez and Big Mijo as an expressive outlet in a community experiencing gang violence. The word stands for Kingdom Radically Uplifted Mighty Praise.

Character: Krump is raw, expressive, and high-energy. It's the most emotionally intense of the street styles — dancers often describe a session as cathartic. Core moves include chest pops (percussive contractions of the chest), heavy stomps, arm swings and jabs from the shoulder, and buck jumps. Krump is primarily a battle style — everything is performed in conversation with an opponent.

How to start: The chest pop is the entry point. Exhale sharply while contracting your pectoral muscles so your chest jolts forward visibly. Add stomps underneath it. Don't worry about looking clean immediately — krump values authenticity and feeling over polish.

Music: Heavy hip-hop instrumentals, trap beats, and gospel-influenced hip-hop. The music should have weight and impact.

6. House

Origin: Chicago, 1980s, born directly from the house music scene at clubs like The Warehouse. The footwork-heavy style reflects the steady 4/4 house beat.

Core moves: House is floor-connected and rhythmically intricate. Jacking is the foundational movement — a continuous forward-and-back pelvic pump that drives the whole body. Footwork patterns include the shuffle, the skate, and the running man. House also incorporates African and Latin influences visible in its loose, polyrhythmic body movements. At its most advanced, house dancers improvise complex footwork phrases over 8-bar phrases.

How to start: Start with jacking — feel the house beat and let it drive your body forward and back. Then add foot patterns. The running man is accessible for beginners and translates directly into more advanced house vocabulary.

Music: House music — Chicago house, deep house, afro-house. The 4/4 kick drum is non-negotiable.

7. Lite Feet

Origin: New York City, early 2000s, emerging from Harlem. Lite Feet is associated with the "Get Lite" movement and has roots in the Harlem Shake (the original, not the 2013 viral meme).

Character: Lite Feet is characterized by lightness — quick, bouncy footwork that makes the dancer look like they're barely touching the ground. Core moves include the Get Lite (a bouncing, rhythmic step with alternating arm swings), the Harlem Shake (shoulder isolation with bounce), and various stomping-and-bouncing combinations. Lite Feet is social and communal — it thrives in cyphers and trains.

How to start: Learn the bounce rhythm first — a consistent vertical energy in every step. Then add the Get Lite step: shift your weight from foot to foot while keeping the bounce alive and swinging your arms opposite to your feet.

Music: New York hip-hop, especially drill and boom-bap with a strong kick pattern.

8. Tutting

Origin: Evolved from popping culture in the 1980s and 1990s, with the name referencing ancient Egyptian hieroglyphic poses. Finger tutting emerged as a distinct sub-style in the 2000s with the rise of video culture.

Core technique: Tutting is built entirely from geometric angles — 90-degree and 45-degree positions formed with the arms, hands, and fingers. The discipline is architectural: you create boxes, pyramids, and planes in space. Arm tutting uses the elbows and wrists to form squares and L-shapes. Finger tutting uses individual finger joints to build miniature geometric structures, often in front of the camera for close-up viewing.

How to start: Hold both arms out in front of you, bent at 90 degrees at the elbow, forming a box shape. Practice transitioning from one box configuration to another smoothly. Every move should create a clean, readable angle — no curved wrists, no sloppy elbows. Once arm shapes are clean, start working individual fingers.

Music: Electronic music — glitchy, precise, and rhythmically complex. Aphex Twin, Glitch Mob, and IDM producers.

Which Style Should You Start With?

If you want structure and acrobatics: Breaking. If you love musicality and precision: Popping. If you want personality and fun: Locking. If you love drama and fashion: Waacking. If you want raw emotional expression: Krump. If you love footwork and rhythm: House. If you want something social and accessible: Lite Feet. If you want a challenge that looks incredible on camera: Tutting.

The best advice is to try at least three before committing. Each one will teach you something different about your own movement instincts — and those instincts will tell you where to go next.
